## Why a court shoe

Pickleball on a hard court is a series of **lateral cuts, split steps, and stops**, then a quiet reset at the kitchen. The outsole and the heel have to hold that pattern. A road-running shoe is built to roll forward. It is the wrong tool for the stop, even when it feels soft in a straight line.

What this women’s shortlist actually prints, instead of a lab score we do not have:

- **Court Express** — AOSTA II, a high-density **non-marking** outsole the listing says is durable from heel to toe, plus K-EVA cushioning and an OrthoLite sock liner that “keeps feet cool and minimizes the effects of perspiration.”
- **Express Light** — DragGuard 7.0 rubber at the toes and heels for pivots and drags, and a wide toe box. The Durawrap upper line says a tough overlay fights toe-dragging wear and stays airy for long tennis or volleyball sessions. That is the listing’s durability language, not a pickleball-only wear test.
- **Hypercourt Express 2** — AOSTA 7.0 high-density rubber from heel to toe, plus a midfoot chassis (below). The product **name** still says tennis shoe.
- **Viper Court** — the description prints **Goodyear rubber** for indoor and outdoor hard courts, with Arch Fit support. The feature line is cut off mid-word after “Goodye,” so the description is the sentence we use.
- **Viper Court Rally** — a **Goodyear Gold** compound the listing says delivers traction, stability, and durability, on a padel-friendly court design.
- **FitVille wide women** — “court-ready traction for pickleball and badminton,” with a cushioned build for longer sessions.

Tennis court shoes often transfer to pickleball; running shoes do not. That split is spelled out in [pickleball vs tennis](/guides/pickleball-vs-tennis). None of these six listings print a gram weight, a drop, or a stack height. “Lighter” on Express Light and Rally is the catalog’s word. It is not a scale result.
